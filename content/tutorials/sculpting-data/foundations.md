Data comes from outside: a recording, a picture, a model, a film, a microphone, a camera. MayaFlux can play it as it is, or take it apart and rebuild it into something its maker never heard or saw.

How to use these cards:

- Pick the kind of data you want to work with: sound, picture, film, a model
- Run card 1 to bring it in
- Jump to the card that works on it, and run its first snippet
- Change one number at a time, and run it again
- Open the explanations when you can follow what a change did, and change it again with more reach
- Finish a card with **Try It → Recap**, for wilder examples and exercises
- Then pick another kind of data and repeat

Some things take one line. Playing a recording does, and card 1 shows it. Other things don't, and we won't pretend otherwise: a fire you can feed with your hand, that answers a sound, is a piece of work a single line cannot hold. One line would show you a fire. It could not let you play with one.

So the longer blocks are not hiding the difficulty. They are the shortest working version of the thing, with the interaction already in it. Copy one and run it. Change a number and watch what moves. You can use all of it without understanding any of it, and read the code when you want to know why.

If you are going to paste something you don't yet follow, paste this, and not a handful of triangles. A shape with nothing to answer to teaches you where the edge is. A fire you can reach into teaches you what is possible.

If a word or a call does not make sense yet, run the code and carry on. A later card often explains an earlier one better.

*Form is data. It can come from anywhere, and it can go anywhere.*

You have reached the end, which means you ran things. That was the method.

What to carry out of here is not a list of names. `create_sampler`, `grain_size` and `set_looping` will fade, and each is a search away. What should stay is a feel for what a number does to a sound, a picture, a shape or a fire, the way a player knows an instrument without reciting its parts.

Two kinds of understanding are false, and both feel real. One is the English meaning of a name. A function called `smooth` or `pluck` suggests a story, and the story is easy to believe and often slightly wrong. The other is sight and sound moving together. When a picture bends as a sound swells it seems you can see why, but two things changing at once only show that they are tied, not how. Neither is knowledge until it predicts something.

So test it. Before you change a number, say what you expect to hear or see, then run it. If you were right, push the number further: to zero, to ten times, below zero. If you were wrong, you have found the part worth learning. Change one thing at a time. Break a working block on purpose. Swap its source, the file for the microphone, and see what stays.

Understanding comes from that loop, repeated until you stop predicting and simply know. Then put the blocks together: a pointer on a pile of pictures, a drone steering a shape. Nothing in these sections was written to stay as it was.
